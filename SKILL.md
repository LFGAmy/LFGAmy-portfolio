## How I work

**Five things I build into every function — each one makes the next stronger:**

- **Builder community fluency.** Years embedded in developer infrastructure roles (Coinbase Cloud, Eigen Labs) plus founding a SaaS product means I'm in the room with builders as a peer. I speak the language, understand the values, and know what makes technical audiences show up versus quietly opt out.

- **Brand authority instinct.** Field marketing and events only work when they reinforce brand. Every activation I run is designed to compound the company's brand in its market — not just generate leads. This means thinking about brand systems, not just events.

- **First-principles experiential design.** Original formats — never borrowed, always grounded in real tools and standards. I invent formats that work for the specific audience and moment — hacker houses with hand-curated cohorts, executive programs designed around real builder problems, activations that produce shipped artifacts. Memorable moments that compound.

- **Global execution — NA, LATAM, EMEA, APAC, and Australia.** I've shipped event programs across every major region. Multi-region work isn't just logistics — it's cultural fluency, time zone-aware program design, partner relationships across markets, and adapting brand voice without losing identity. For platforms serving global developer audiences, this matters.

- **Documentation-first operating model.** I work in the open. Every framework, brief, forecast, run-of-show, and recap lives in a structured home base anyone on the team can access at any time. Knowledge doesn't live in my head — it lives in the system. If I left tomorrow, the team would inherit a working function, not a guessing game. Most field marketers and event leads carry critical context internally and call it "tribal knowledge." I refuse to. This compounds team trust and protects the company from the turnover risk that quietly kills programs.

## Events are the real-world trust layer

If you build an event and no one shows up, you don't have an event. And if you build one where all you're thinking about is what you'll gain — not what you'll give — you've wasted time and money. Either way, it comes back to the same thing: the people are the event. You need the people to fill the room. You need the people to provide the data. You need the people for the conversion.

So the promise is simple. Stop thinking about what you need, and start thinking about what you can give. Trust is built when the service runs both ways — and it lives in the details and the care you put into the experience.
